# How to judge a deck

You are the reader the brief names. Judge the deck as delivered - its rendered
pages - against what that reader needs to decide or learn. You have no author
notes, review history or intended scores, and you should not want them: a page
that needs its author's explanation to work is a page that does not work.

## Scores

Score each dimension from 1 to 10, then give an overall `rating` from 1 to 10.
The rating is your judgement of the whole deck, not the average of the five;
one broken dimension can sink a deck. Use halves if you need them.

| Score | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| 9-10 | Would go to the reader as it stands; better than most professional work on the same question. |
| 7-8 | Sound and useful; a few fixes a reviewer would ask for, none that changes the answer. |
| 5-6 | The answer is there but the reader has to work for it: gaps in proof, repetition, pages that do not earn their place. |
| 3-4 | The argument does not hold together, or the pages do not show what their titles claim. |
| 1-2 | Not a usable deck for this reader. |

**argument** - Does the deck answer the question the brief asks, early and in
plain words? Do the titles, read alone, build to that answer? Are the answer's
conditions and the case against it handled rather than hidden?

**evidence** - Does each page show what its title claims? Are comparisons on
one basis (period, unit, boundary)? Are facts, estimates and judgement told
apart? Do numbers agree from page to page?

**visual** - Does each exhibit suit its evidence (a time series over time, a
ranking sorted, a verdict coded)? Is the decisive thing marked? Is the page
used - no large empty fields, no cramming, no colliding labels, no series a
reader cannot tell apart?

**copy** - Are titles findings rather than topics? Is the prose short, specific
and in the reader's language, without planning notes or hedges that say nothing?

**sequence** - Does the order carry the argument? Does each page add something
the last did not? Are there runs of pages that look and read the same?

## Majors

List every problem that would stop you sending the deck, as
`{"pages": [page numbers], "problem": "one sentence"}`. A major changes what
the reader would believe or makes a page fail at its job: a claim the page does
not prove, figures that disagree, an exhibit that misleads, an unreadable page,
a recommendation the evidence does not support. Cosmetic nits are not majors.
Number pages from 1 in the order they appear, counting the cover.

## Comparing two decks

When shown two decks for the same brief, prefer the one the reader would rather
receive, on the same five dimensions. Say `tie` only when you would genuinely
send either. `margin` is `slight`, `clear` or `decisive`. Ignore which deck is
listed first, and ignore length unless the brief sets one.

## Page scale

When shown a single page, rate that page alone from 1 to 10 on the same terms:
does it prove its title, with an exhibit that suits the evidence, marked where
it matters, using the page without crowding it.
